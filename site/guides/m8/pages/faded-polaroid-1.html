The mood

A photo found in a drawer: the shapes are all there, but the edges have gone soft and the light has flattened. Hollow little plucks circling through ninth chords, a round bass, a breathy flute-like voice that keeps reaching for a note and settling one step short of home, and a beat that sounds taped off the radio. Calm and fond, a little sad, never heavy. For the feel: Boards of Canada's "Roygbiv" (a simple, wobbly lead over warm chords), Tycho's "Awake" (major-key ninth chords, nothing hurried), Washed Out's "Feel It All Around" (hazy, slowed, tape-soft).

Your four bars

One chord a bar, I, V, vi, IV. Three things make it sound the way it does.

Rootless chords

Your instrument 10 plays four notes, and a ninth chord has five. So it leaves the root out and plays the four above it: F♯ A C♯ E over a D is Dmaj9. The bass track supplies the root. This is the same idea Low Tide's pad uses, and it's why the chords sound open and a little unanchored until the bass comes in.

Ninths on top

Every chord's top note is its own ninth: E over D, B over A, C♯ over B, A over G. The lead you write in step 6 comes to rest on ninths too. A ninth is a note that wants to move and is allowed not to, which is most of the song's nostalgia.

The arp lives inside the chords

Your arp runs eighth notes through each chord's upper notes, between G3 and F♯5: the same register as the chords. It's the song's motion. Later, under the lead, it steps back (quieter, with gaps) rather than moving to another octave.
