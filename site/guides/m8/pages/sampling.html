Technique

Sampling

The Sampler instrument plays a WAV file from the SD card, records one, edits it, and cuts it into slices. This page goes in the order you'd meet it: loading a sample and the pitch limit; start, loop and length; slices; recording on the M8; resampling the M8's own tracks; and the Zoom M4 route for pitching a sound a long way down.

1. Load a sample, and the pitch limit

  1. On a free instrument, set TYPE to SAMPLER. Press EDIT on LOAD, to the right of SAMPLE, to open the file browser.
  2. Directories start with /. PLAY previews the highlighted file; EDIT loads it; OPTION leaves.
  3. Once one is loaded, EDIT+LEFT/RIGHT on the sample's name steps through the other files in its folder, a quick way to audition.

Only uncompressed PCM WAV, mono or stereo, 8 to 32 bit, with a path under 128 characters. Because samples stream from the SD card, notes above C-4 are limited by bit depth and channels. If a sample won't play high, make it smaller in the sample editor: MONO, 16-BIT, or DOWNSAMPLE (half the rate). 16-bit is the manual's recommendation.

Which note plays a sample at its own pitch. The pitch limit starting above C-4 suggests C-4 is where a sample plays as recorded; the Low Tide pages use it. Report back: does a sample sound at its own pitch on C-4?

2. Start, loop, length

  • START is where playback begins, LEN how much plays. Both can be moved by FX commands, which is how one long sample becomes many sounds.
  • PLAY picks direction and looping: FWD, REV, FWDLOOP, REVLOOP, the ping-pong modes (FWD PP, REV PP), and the OSC modes, which ignore START and don't restart on a new note.
  • LOOP ST. is where a looping mode goes back to. The sample editor's XFADE LOOP smooths the seam.
  • DETUNE: 80 is in tune; the first digit moves semitones, the second sixteenths of one.
  • REPITCH/BPM plays a loop at the song's tempo by changing its pitch; STEPS/BPM tells the M8 how long the loop is.

3. Slices

Slices cut a sample into pieces you play from the note column: the first slice on C-1, the second on C#1, and on up. Two ways to make them:

  • SLICE in the instrument view cuts the sample into equal lengths.
  • Markers, saved in the WAV file. In the sample editor, PROCESS has SLICE:AUTO (finds the hits), SLICE:SILENC (cuts at the gaps) and SLICE:[0–128] (equal divisions). Or chop by ear: with the cursor on a slice marker, press PLAY, then tap EDIT on each hit ("lazy chop"). Then set SLICE to 01 FILE to play the markers.

A sliced drum loop re-ordered: slices 1 to 5 on C-1 to E-1, played in a new pattern, with velocity for accents. The SLI command cues a slice from the FX column instead.

SLICE's values. The manual names 01 FILE for markers and says SLICE also makes equal sections, but not what the other values read. Report back: what does SLICE show for the first few values after 01?

4. Record on the M8

EDIT on REC. opens the sample editor ready to record. Low Tide's step 8 walks through a field recording on the mic. The parts that matter:

  • SRC: MIC (Model:02), the line inputs (L&R, INL, INR), USB, ALL, or a track (TR1 to TR8, for an External Instrument's audio).
  • VOL sets the input level; ARM waits until the input crosses a threshold before it starts, so you don't trim silence.
  • Start with the power button to keep the key click out of a mic recording.
  • Then SELECT, PROCESS (CROP, NORMALIZE, FADE IN/OUT, XFADE LOOP, REVERSE…), NAME, SAVE. OPTION leaves the editor.

5. Resample the M8 itself

You can turn any part of the song into a sample, then chop it, reverse it, or pitch it like any other.

  1. In the Song view, SHIFT+OPTION and move to select the rows and tracks you want.
  2. Double-tap EDIT. Choose QUICK RENDER (offline, the tracks only) or REALTIME (also records the line and USB inputs).
  3. A new instrument appears holding the sample, and it becomes the default for new notes.

Low Tide idea: resample the pad's eight bars, reverse the sample, and use it as a swell into the breakdown.

For files to take home, Project view, RENDER writes a mix or one file per track to /Renders.

6. Pitching down a long way: the Zoom M4 route

The M8 plays a sample lower by playing it slower, which also takes its top end down. An octave down, a sample recorded at 44.1 kHz has nothing left above about 11 kHz, and sounds dull and grainy. For a sound you mean to pitch down a lot (a voice into a drone, a cymbal into a wash):

  1. Record it on the Zoom M4 at 96 kHz, 32-bit float. That captures detail up to about 48 kHz, well past hearing, and float can't clip.
  2. On the desktop, transpose it down. An octave down at 96 kHz still has content up to about 24 kHz: the whole audible range.
  3. Export at 44.1 or 48 kHz, 16-bit, mono if it doesn't need to be stereo, and copy it to the SD card's /Samples folder.
  4. On the M8, play it near its own pitch. The heavy lifting has been done.

For the M8's own mic recordings on the trip, none of this is needed: they're for textures that sit low in the mix, where a little dullness is welcome.
